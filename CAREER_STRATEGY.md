# Career Strategy -- Public-Safety Technology First

This document is the durable decision standard for Troy J. Hokanson's job search. Use it before selecting a profile, tailoring a resume, choosing professional development, or deciding how to spend job-search time.

The chosen direction is a full-time civilian role with a public-safety technology vendor. The strongest fit is a senior individual-contributor position that uses Troy's investigative background, technical credibility, teaching experience, and understanding of agency operations to help customers adopt and use technology successfully.

This is a career pivot back toward Troy's real professional interests, not one option among equally weighted paths. Investigations remain a viable fallback lane. Formal people-manager roles are selective, not the center of the search.

---

## Primary Target

Prioritize agency-facing roles at companies that serve law enforcement, public safety, courts, government investigations, digital forensics, evidence management, intelligence, emergency response, or adjacent regulated markets.

Target role families include:

- Customer Success Manager, Senior Customer Success Manager, Strategic Customer Success Manager
- Implementation Consultant, Senior Implementation Consultant, Implementation Manager
- Professional Services Consultant and Technical Consultant
- Solutions Consultant, Solutions Engineer, Sales Engineer, and Field Application Specialist
- Customer Training Consultant, Enablement Consultant, and Learning Program Manager
- Customer Program Manager, Agency Success Manager, and Customer Engagement Manager
- Technical Account Manager and Strategic Account Manager when the role is consultative rather than quota-led
- Public Safety Advisor, Law Enforcement Subject-Matter Expert, and Public Safety Strategy roles
- Product Owner, Product Specialist, Product Operations, and practitioner-informed product strategy roles

Titles containing "manager" are in scope when the work centers on customers, implementations, programs, accounts, or outcomes rather than direct reports.

### Best-fit work

The strongest roles ask Troy to:

- Translate agency workflows into product, implementation, or training requirements
- Build credibility with detectives, officers, analysts, supervisors, prosecutors, IT teams, and executives
- Demonstrate or explain investigative technology to technical and non-technical audiences
- Lead onboarding, adoption, training, workshops, or multi-agency rollouts
- Gather field feedback and carry the voice of the customer to product and engineering teams
- Coordinate complex customer issues across sales, product, support, legal, and operations
- Help agencies connect product capabilities to measurable operational outcomes

## Parallel and Selective Lanes

### Investigations as a parallel lane

Investigations remain a credible option, especially when the role values complex casework, financial crimes, digital evidence, interviews, report writing, interagency coordination, and mature judgment. Relevant targets include corporate investigations, fraud investigations, financial crimes investigations, insider threat, trust and safety, program integrity, and investigations or intelligence analysis.

This lane may remain active without displacing the vendor goal or creating a large study burden. An investigations role deserves attention when it is a strong substantive match, offers a sustainable civilian career path, and uses Troy's established strengths without requiring a costly reinvention.

### People management as a selective lane

Consider team-lead or people-manager roles when the job emphasizes coaching, mentoring, training, operational judgment, customer escalations, program leadership, process improvement, and public-safety expertise.

Deprioritize roles that depend heavily on several years of formal responsibility for hiring, termination, performance ratings, compensation, workforce planning, large-team management, departmental budgets, or revenue forecasting. Apply only when the rest of the fit is unusually strong and the employer clearly values functional leadership.

## Positioning Standard

Troy is not an entry-level corporate candidate and should not be presented as a long-tenured people manager. He is a senior practitioner moving his experience into a civilian, agency-facing setting.

I understand how agencies evaluate change, how investigators use technology under real operational pressure, and how to translate complex workflows for technical and non-technical audiences. I am targeting a senior individual-contributor role where I can help public-safety customers adopt technology and achieve operational outcomes.

### Leadership language

Use accurate, specific forms of leadership:

- Technical leadership through digital-forensics and investigative subject-matter expertise
- Operational leadership in complex, high-accountability environments
- Investigative leadership through case coordination, evidence strategy, and sound judgment
- Instructional leadership through curriculum development, facilitation, coaching, and feedback
- Cross-functional leadership across agencies, prosecutors, technical specialists, administrators, students, and partner organizations

Do not use broad claims such as "25 years of law-enforcement leadership" without immediately defining the type of leadership involved.

## Job Evaluation Framework

Score each serious opportunity from 0 to 2 on the eight factors below:

| Factor | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mission and market | Little connection to public safety or investigations | Adjacent regulated or government market | Public-safety, justice, forensics, evidence, or agency technology |
| Practitioner advantage | Prior experience adds little | Transferable experience helps | End-user and agency credibility is a central advantage |
| Work content | Mostly unrelated administration or generic sales | Some relevant customer or investigative work | Customer adoption, implementation, solutions, training, strategy, or substantive investigations |
| Senior IC fit | Role level is a reset or unclear | Reasonable level with some gaps | Senior individual-contributor scope with ownership and influence |
| Leadership accuracy | Requires extensive formal people management | Leadership requirement is mixed | Values technical, operational, instructional, or cross-functional leadership |
| Skills bridge | Major unsupported requirements | Learnable commercial or technical gaps | Existing strengths map directly and gaps can be closed quickly |
| Sustainability | Material concerns about travel, schedule, culture, or demands | Tradeoffs require discussion | Work pattern and expectations appear sustainable |
| Career direction | Pulls away from the long-term goal | Neutral or preserves options | Builds vendor credibility, customer-facing experience, product knowledge, or a strong investigations path |

### Decision bands

- **13-16: Priority.** Tailor carefully, seek a referral or direct contact, and apply promptly.
- **9-12: Selective.** Apply when the largest gaps are learnable and the role advances the primary or parallel lane.
- **0-8: Deprioritize.** Do not let a recognizable employer or salary consume time better spent on stronger fits.

An automatic warning applies when the job is primarily quota ownership, demands extensive direct-report history, requires unsupported credentials as a hard gate, or offers little connection to either public-safety technology or substantive investigations.

## Day-to-Day Operating Rhythm

Use the primary goal to allocate attention, not a rigid application quota.

### Weekly priorities

1. Search public-safety and investigative-technology vendors first.
2. Score promising postings before tailoring anything.
3. Spend the most application time on priority roles, including company research, a tailored resume, and a credible outreach message.
4. Maintain a smaller investigations pipeline for unusually strong financial-crimes, corporate-investigations, digital-investigations, or intelligence roles.
5. Review selective lead or manager roles only after confirming that formal direct-report experience is not the main qualification.
6. Record recurring skill gaps from real job postings and use those patterns to choose learning investments.

### Suggested time allocation

- 50 percent: targeted applications, networking, and follow-up for public-safety vendor roles
- 20 percent: vendor and product research, demonstrations, portfolio evidence, and interview preparation
- 15 percent: practical skill building tied to recurring vendor-role requirements
- 10 percent: unusually strong investigations opportunities that use existing experience
- 5 percent: CFE study only when a specific opportunity justifies it under the decision rule below

Adjust the mix when live opportunities demand it, but do not allow a secondary credential to become a substitute for applications, conversations, portfolio work, or product fluency.

## CFE Decision Rule

The Certified Fraud Examiner credential is useful for certain investigation, SIU, program-integrity, banking, fintech, and financial-crimes roles. It is not the default priority for the public-safety vendor lane, and extensive CFE study is not the career Troy wants to organize his time around.

The default decision is to pause or minimize CFE study and put that time into vendor applications, networking, product knowledge, demonstrations, portfolio evidence, and customer-facing interview preparation. CFE study becomes an exception that must earn its place on the calendar.

Continue or intensify CFE study only when at least one of these conditions is true:

- Two or more high-fit, actively pursued roles list CFE as required or preferred
- The current application pipeline is intentionally weighted toward fraud or financial-crimes investigations
- A target employer or recruiter confirms that the credential would materially improve candidacy
- A scheduled exam and realistic study plan make completion a bounded near-term investment
- Study is not reducing the quality or speed of priority vendor applications, networking, interview preparation, or relevant product learning

Keep CFE study paused or minimal when:

- It is driven mainly by guilt, sunk cost, or the feeling that another credential is needed before applying
- Current priority roles value customer engagement, implementation, training, product knowledge, or public-safety credibility more than fraud certification
- The same time could produce a stronger vendor application, referral conversation, product demonstration, portfolio artifact, or interview story
- Few recent target postings mention the credential

Ask one practical question each week:

> What is the best evidence that one hour of CFE study will move the current job search further than one hour spent on a priority vendor opportunity?

If there is no concrete evidence, put the hour toward the public-safety vendor goal. Troy does not need to become a credential-first fraud specialist to make a strong civilian transition. Pausing CFE study preserves fraud investigations as an option while keeping daily effort aligned with the work he actually wants.

## Guardrails

- Do not market Troy only as a retired detective. Lead with the value he brings to the target role.
- Do not claim conventional people-management experience he has not held.
- Do not describe transferable experience as identical to SaaS or commercial experience.
- Do not hide the learning curve. Name it precisely and show how it is being closed.
- Do not pursue credentials merely to delay applying.
- Do not let broad job-search activity replace focused work on employers where Troy's practitioner background is genuinely differentiated.
- Keep adjunct teaching visible. Eighteen-plus years of adult instruction is direct evidence of communication, facilitation, curriculum development, coaching, audience adaptation, and sustained professional credibility.
